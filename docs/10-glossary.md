# Glossary

> Plain-English definitions, in alphabetical order, for every term this
> handbook leans on. Definitions are short on purpose: if any entry needs
> more than a screen, a chapter teaches it properly (linked below).

## Double opt-in

New subscribers must click a confirmation link before joining the list
for real. Slightly more friction, (much) cleaner list. Chapter 3 turns
this on.

## Open rate

The percentage of delivered emails that were opened. Treat small-list
numbers as noise, trends over months as signal; some privacy features
inflate or hide opens, so never build decisions on any single month.

## Bounce, hard and soft

A bounce is a failed delivery. A **hard** bounce means the address is
gone forever (typo, abandoned account); a **soft** bounce means "not
right now" (full inbox, busy server). Providers count these automatically;
repeated hard bounces should be removed from your list.

## CTA

Call to action -- the one thing an email asks the reader to do next:
read the post, buy the book, reply to the question. One per email
(chapter 4's rule; more becomes a to-do list).

## Cadence

How often you send and on what rhythm. Chapter 7's whole thesis: start
monthly, same-day-of-month, and make the promise protect itself.

## CAN-SPAM / GDPR

Two of the laws shaping commercial email: CAN-SPAM (US) requires a real
physical postal address and working unsubscribes; GDPR (EU) requires
clear, specific consent before you email someone. Both are handled by
the settings in chapter 3 -- honest newsletters rarely strain against
either; dishonest ones always do.

## Deliverability

Whether your email actually reaches inboxes rather than spam folders.
Configured once with SPF/DKIM/DMARC (chapter 3), maintained forever with
list hygiene (chapter 8) and moderation (all chapters).

## DKIM

A cryptographic signature your provider adds to outgoing mail so
receivers can verify it was not forged or altered. One of the three DNS
records in chapter 3.

## DMARC

A policy record telling inboxes how strictly to treat mail that claims
to come from you, and where to send the reports. Optional in theory,
near-mandatory since Google and Yahoo got strict in 2024 (chapter 3).

## One-click out

The rule for leaving: one click on the unsubscribe link, one
confirmation page, done. Friction on the way OUT is a complaint-rate
multiplier (chapter 8's never list).

## ESP

Email Service Provider: the company that stores your list and sends your
mail (chapter 2). You cannot run a newsletter properly without one.

## From name

The name shown as the sender in the inbox. **A person's name beats a
brand name** for author newsletters, consistently (chapter 3 sets it;
chapter 4 explains why it matters in welcome metrics).

## Lead magnet

Something useful and free -- a checklist, printable, first chapter --
handed out in exchange for a signup. Built once, shipped forever
(chapter 8).

## List hygiene

Keeping the list of subscribers clean: removal of dead addresses and
gone-quiet ghost cohorts via honest re-permission. Good hygiene is
anti-dread and pro-deliverability (both chapters 7 and 8 agree).

## Preheader

The snippet of preview text most mail programs show after the subject
line. Set it on purpose: chapter 5's second sentence.

## Segmentation

Sending different content to different slices of your list (say,
finished-book readers versus curious browsers). Nice-to-have later;
harmful complexity at the start. Not needed for chapters 1-9's
path.

## SPF

A DNS record that publishes which computers may send email as you --
the "who is allowed" list from chapter 3.

## Swipe file

A personal collection of writing you admire -- especially your own best
lines -- kept in one file for reuse and (with AI, in chapter 6) as your
style reference -- a better "voice sample" than anything generic.

## Welcome sequence

The automatic emails greeting new subscribers (chapter 4). The highest
open rates of anything you will ever send; the reason chapter 6's AI
loop has such strict no-can guidelines.

## Whitelist / safe-sender note

Asking a reader to mark you as safe (or star the sender). Light-touch
requests in chapter 4's welcome email #4; a real deliverability
multiplier you must ask for, because inbox filters listen to readers.
