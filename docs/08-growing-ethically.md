# Growing Your List Ethically

> Last reviewed: October 2026. Growth channels come and go; consent law
> and list hygiene change slowly. Both are handled together here because
> your sender reputation depends on both: bad growth is how even
> well-written newsletters end up spam-folded (chapter 3's filters keep
> score forever).

Chapter 8 is the anti-growth-hacking chapter. The entire strategy is one
sentence: **grow the list the way you would grow a friendship -- invited,
at a human pace, with no escalations.** That sounds soft until you see
the numbers it protects (chapter 3's spam-complaint threshold) and the
years it buys.

## The never list (the actual rules)

Do these things, even once, and you can lose deliverability and sometimes
the whole provider account:

- **Never buy, borrow, scrape, or "enrich" a list.** Every person on your
  list must have asked for it, related to *this* newsletter. Bought lists
  are why the spam folder exists.
- **Never add someone because you know them.** A friend agreeing to
  coffee is not consent to be subscribed. Invite them instead.
- **Never hide the unsubscribe.** No broken or buried links, no
  "reply to leave no one reads," no emotional guilt walls in the footer.
  Making leaving easy keeps complaint rates tiny and looks good to
  every inbox filter.
- **Never promise one thing and send another.** If the form said "monthly
  AI tools," a weekly sales funnel is list theft. Chapter 7's promise
  rule is also a consent rule.
- **Never rinse a stale list into a fresh send.** See the
  re-permission section below for the gentle, correct version of the
  thing some marketers call "we're cleaning the list" -- which in
  practice is often a disguised set of dunning mail to strangers.

## The honest channels (what actually works)

Steady, unglamorous, and all free:

- **Back matter.** Every book, PDF, or free guide you make gets one page
  at the end: "Liked this? One email a month, plain English, here is the
  link." An ebook on KDP can carry that link as a text page; no paid
  tier of anything is needed to do this.
- **Every profile you already have.** Author bios, GitHub (this
  handbook's own project family is full of such links -- see the
  pairing note on the landing page), marketplace pages, and the genuine sentence every author gets:
  "do you have anything I can just read?"
- **One public home post per month.** Issue day goes same-day to the
  feed or blog where readers already know you. You are not selling; you
  are saying "here is this month's issue, door is open."
- **Lead magnets that earn their keep.** A free checklist, printable, or
  first-chapter PDF. Build once, hand out forever -- the
  free-author-printables family works the same way. One caution: never
  gate a lead magnet behind anything hard to leave (that is consent
  theater).
- **Cross-promotion and swaps, scrupulously honest.** Recommend a peer
  whose newsletter you genuinely read, when a natural slot fits the
  issue. Engineered high-volume swap networks train readers to distrust
  the gesture; one honest recommendation carries more weight.
- **Replies and word-of-mouth.** The quiet channel from chapter 4: every
  real reply thread often ends with "can I share this?" -- and one
  reader-vouch-sent-forward outcompetes a week's worth of advertising.
  Reply-subscribers are your most durable cohort.
- **Podcasting, talks, guest spots, instructor meals** -- whatever you
  already speak at; one signup line and no over-follow-up.

## The welcome sequence is the retention engine

Chapter 8 is about growth, but growth's quieter half is stopping the
leak: a two-to-three times better opening rate during your welcome
window is also the window where a wrong signup decides whether to leave
(chapter 4's four emails are engineered to be easily worth keeping).
Retention is this chapter's most free, most ethical growth lever, and it
is the only one that compounds while you sleep.

## List hygiene (good hygiene is anti-dread and pro-deliverability)

- **Let go of ghosts without sadness.** Six-plus months silent, no opens,
  never clicked once = a candidate for removal -- ideally via one clean,
  honest "still want this?" re-permission email, sent *once*, with easy
  yes and even-easier no. This is the honest core inside the otherwise
  abused "re-permission" tactic; honest here means: no bribe, no
  deadline countdown, no third reminder.
- **Watch two numbers, weekly, for five minutes:** opens trend (is my
  list warming or cooling?) and complaint rate (basically must stay
  near zero; spam-complaint thresholds above ~0.1-0.3% of sends hurt
  delivery for everyone, which is why bought lists are poison).
- **Prune before you notice it's needed.** A quarterly 20-minute prune
  session (calendar note) keeps the dip invisible.
- **Keep the archive separate from the list forever** -- a subscribing
  reader is a person, not a ranking; chapter 9's local files make your
  issues livable beyond the account.

## Small-list morale (because the numbers lie to beginners)

A few calibrations, since every newsletter starts at zero:

- **12 real readers is an event.** A dozen humans whom an email can
  genuinely reach is more distribution than many authors used to have
  in their whole careers.
- **The classic author-newsletter curve is uneven, not smooth** --
  quiet months, then a book-launch or a talk puts 40 new names in a
  week. Growth is punctuated. Design the promise (chapter 7) to need
  no particular curve.
- **Reply count > subscriber count, forever.** One reader replying "I
  finally understood SPF because of this" outranks a hundred ghost
  opens. This is chapter 6's honesty passing chapter 4's reply engine.
