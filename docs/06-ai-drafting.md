# Drafting With AI Without Sounding Canned

Chapter 6 is about the tool most of this handbook's readers are curious
about, using a bias stated up front: **AI is a good junior writing
partner and a terrible ghostwriter-with-final-say.** Used as a Rubber
Duck with a keyboard, it saves hours. Used as "make it sound nice," it
produces the exact flattened newsletter voice every reader is now
trained to skim-skip.

This chapter is deliberately the longest. It covers the loop, the edits,
the tells, and the ethics, and it assumes both of its end states are
failure modes: a newsletter that sounds like a machine, and a newsletter
that never ships because you refused all help.

## Why AI drafts go canned (then the fix)

AI language models are trained on enormous amounts of published text,
which means their default register is *average-of-everything*: agreeable,
energetic, and structurally identical from essay to essay. You have
read this voice: "In a world where technology is rapidly evolving..."
Reader brains detect it in about a sentence.

The fix is not to avoid AI. It is to keep two things firmly yours:

1. **The raw material** -- your notes, your stories, your opinions, your
   weird specific details. AI cannot invent what actually happened to
   you, and authenticity *is* the raw material.
2. **The final voice pass** -- a human read in which you cut every line
   "nobody actually says" (more below: the banned list).

Everything between those two can be delegated freely.

## The working loop (works in any AI chat tool, free or local)

A repeatable five-step loop, timed against the four-pass pipeline in
chapter 7: under two hours per issue, split into short sittings.

1. **Feed context, not vibes.** Paste in: (a) a sample of your own
   writing, 300 words or so of something you were proud of, so the model
   copies *your* rhythm; (b) your rough notes for this issue, bullet
   points welcome; (c) the issue's one-sentence promise from the
   [issue template](../templates/issue-template.md).
2. **Ask for an outline, never a draft.** This is the single highest-
   leverage instruction in the chapter, so it is in bold: *"Give me three
   outline options for a 600-word issue on [topic]; keep my voice from
   the sample; no intro paragraph."* Outlines are where AI is strong
   (structure, coverage) and still completely you (the ideas).
3. **Co-write, section by section.** Pick the outline, then go section
   by section: *"Draft the first section in 120 words. If you reach for
   a cliche, stop and ask me what I'd say instead."* Short requests get
   tame prose. Long "write the whole newsletter" requests get the
   average-of-everything voice.
4. **Make it yours.** After each AI section, delete the greeting,
   retype the first sentence in your own words from blank, and add one
   thing only you know (a patient anecdote anonymized, a mistake you
   made, a dollar figure from your own experiment). This ~15-minute
   ritual is the difference between "AI-assisted" and "AI-sounding."
5. **The read-aloud edit.** Print or phone-preview the issue, and read
   it aloud to one human, or the cat, or the empty hallway chair. Mouths
   parse what eyes forgive -- you will hear the canned lines your eyes
   let you skip whole.

## The banned list (a healthy survival instinct)

Kill on sight, in your own final pass:

| Canned phrase family             | Replace with                                    |
| -------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------- |
| "In today's fast-paced world..." | A concrete sentence about your actual week      |
| "delve," "tapestry," "landscape" | The specific noun ("your inbox," "chapter 4's table") |
| "It goes without saying"         | Say it, or cut it                               |
| "game-changer," "unlock"         | What it actually did, with the real outcome     |
| "As an author, I..." openers     | Start mid-thought, in media res                 |
| Triple parallel lists ("X, Y, and Z" stacked A-B-C-D) | Two items. Choose one.       |
| Em-dash chains used as applause  | One dash, where a person would pause            |

Rebuild this list each quarter from your own drafts. The chase is
personal: your tells, not the internet's.

## What AI is genuinely good for here

- **Research summaries you already distrust correctly.** Summarizing a
   long article or docs page you'll then verify -- never publishing
   unverified claims (next section).
- **Variations.** Ten subject-line options from your one-line promise in
  30 seconds; pick by ear (chapter 5).
- **Structure wrestling.** "This draft rambles -- what's the shortest
  defensible order?"
- **The kindness pass.** "Flag sentences a busy nurse at 11pm would skip"
  → cut list. AI is a good editor for mercy.
- **Checklist and table generation** from your prose (like this manual's
  own tables).
- **Translation to reader-English.** Jargon like SPF/DKIM/DMARC into the
  chapter 3 table, with you checking it isn't wrong.

## Hard rules: honesty, privacy, and the copyright brain

- **Never let AI state something you did not verify.** Three named failure
  modes are real and constant: fabricated statistics, invented quotes,
  and confidently wrong citations. If a number appears in your final
  draft, it must have appeared in something you read yourself first. (Chapter 1's table numbers are labeled as rules of thumb for
  this exact reason.)
- **Never paste private reader data into AI tools** -- no names, no
  emails, no "here's an unsubscriber complaint I got." Aggregated,
  de-identified shape only: "a reader replied that..." stays fine as
  long as it is not identifiable. This is the healthcare-worker
  discipline, applied to your list: minimum necessary, always.
- **Disclose when useful, not performative.** A gentle line like
  "drafted with AI assist, edited by me" in an issue occasionally is
  reader-respecting; a disclosure stamp on every paragraph is permission
  theater. The honest default: you owned the voice and the facts, and
  you would be comfortable with both being public.
- **Your swipe file beats their patterns.** Keep a personal file of your
  best-ever sentences (your own, past issues included). Feed that to the
  model before a session instead of generic samples. Your list of your
  own lines is the single best "style guide" that exists, because it is
  the only one about you.

## A worked micro-example

Given the promise "Being remembered beats being seen," a canned AI
opener would write:

> "In today's digital landscape, building meaningful connections with
> your audience is more important than ever..."

The loop version asks for the note first: your notes say "my last feed
post got 2 likes and felt like yelling into the ocean; my last email got
three replies, one of them made me tear up in the break room." Feed *that*.

> "Last Tuesday I posted into a feed, like tossing a pebble into the
> ocean. Same week, an email got three replies, and one of them made me
> stop and stare mid-shift..."

The second one is a newsletter worth receiving, and it took your five
minutes of honesty plus the model's housekeeping. That is the trade this
chapter is selling: AI handles chores; you supply the pebbles.

## When not to use AI

- For your **signature welcome email** if writing it yourself teaches you
  something (rare, but telling).
- For **apology emails** after a mistake: readers can tell,
  and you owe them yourself.
- For anything medical: the standing rule from every Asdale Dynamics
  healthcare-education project -- AI aids *your teaching*, never patient
  care details, and never as a source of medical advice.
