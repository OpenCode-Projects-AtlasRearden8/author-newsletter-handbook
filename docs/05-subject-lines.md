# Subject Lines That Get Opened

> Last reviewed: October 2026. Formulas in this chapter age slower than
> tool facts; character-count guidance is mobile-screen folklore, applied
> lightly.

The subject line is the smallest writing you do with the largest
consequence: it decides whether the whole issue exists to the reader.
Authors consistently under-invest here, then blame "email fatigue" for
misses that were one bad first line.

## The only test

A subject line has one job and one judge. The job: make the right reader
want to open the email from *inside a busy morning*. The judge: whether
you personally would tap that notification. The judge you are writing
against: your own inbox, where things are already arriving at a rate of
roughly one per minute.

## Five rules that do most of the work

1. **Keep it human-short.** Aim mostly below ~45 characters; phones cut
   around there. Short also forces one idea, which is the same reason
   short works ethically: a subject line cannot promise the whole issue.
2. **Specific beats clever.** "How I write 1,200 words with my phone"
   opens; "Words, mysteriously" does not. Curiosity that is not anchored
   in a specific benefit reads as clickbait -- and clickbait spends trust
   you will need later, per chapter 8.
3. **No shouting, no carnival.** ALL CAPS, doubled punctuation ("!!!"),
   and emoji-stacking are the visual language of spam -- and spam filters
   (plus your readers) have learned it. One emoji at the end is
   tolerable in some voices; none is never wrong.
4. **Say the real thing.** Not a curious "This changes everything" -- the actual
   thing: "Why newsletters beat feeds." Refuse to sell certainty you do
   not have. This is the anti-fear-mongering principle, and readers can
   smell the difference.
5. **Write it last, but write it anyway.** Drafting tips: write the
   subject before the body to force specificity, then rewrite it *after*
   the body to keep it honest about what you actually say.

## The preheader is the second sentence

Modern mail clients show a snippet of text after the subject (the
**preheader**). If you set nothing, providers usually grab the first
words of the email -- often a utility link like "View in browser."
Set it on purpose instead: the subject's sidekick, not its echo. Pair them like a tiny
two-panel story:

> **Subject:** The one setting most authors never change
> **Preheader:** Your reply-to address decides who a reply reaches.

Twenty minutes per issue from now on for a 2-3x thinking-quality upgrade
on the first thing everyone reads.

## Formulas (with worked examples)

Steal these shapes; the worked examples are themed for consistency with
this handbook's project, then make them yours:

- **The specific question.** "Why does no one comment on indie books?"
  "What would you do with 10 free subscribers?"
- **The numbered promise.** "3 free tools I actually used last week"
  "2 welcome emails you can steal today"
- **The confession.** "I broke the only rule I asked you to follow" (when
  true -- confessions spend credibility if false, per chapter 8)
- **The plain announcement.** "Issue #7: The Massive AI Manual, chapter
  by chapter" -- named things beat vague things ("Some updates").
- **The name series.** Once your issues have a consistent format, put the
  format's name in it: "Toolkit Tuesday: free landing pages" -- a
  recurring, recognizable prefix trains readers over months.
- **The question-personal.** "What should the glossary define next?"
  Weirdly effective; the reply-eligibility is the point (chapter 4's
  reply engine at work).

The full set of 25, plus what to pair in the preheader for each, lives in

## Testing, sized honestly

- Fine to do **manual A/B** between two variants *before* scheduling,
  by eyeball DMs or the cat test. Do not automate.
- **If your provider offers automated A/B testing on free tiers:** use it
  on the *subject* split only when your list passes a few hundred
  subscribers; under that, the math is noise and the complexity costs
  writing time.
- Whatever you ship, note the result next to the subject line in the
  issue calendar (see [the calendar](../templates/issue-calendar.md)) --
  after a year you will know your own pattern, which beats every listicle.

## The honest hard part

Good subject lines come from specific issues, not from subject-line
skills. "3 welcome emails drafted like a friend" works because someone
actually drafted three emails. When an issue feels mushy, the subject
line rarely fixes it. That diagnosis chains back to the
[issue template](../templates/issue-template.md): it asks for the
one-sentence promise before anything else, and the correct subject line
usually falls out of that sentence with the personality intact.

## Words worth a second look (not a ban list)

No banned-word folklore here -- "free," "hi," and question marks do not
mysteriously trigger filters. What actually receives the rough treatment:
CAPS overuse, emoji bombardment, missing authentication (chapter 3's
 SPF/DKIM work), terrible list hygiene (buying lists; chapter 8), and
misleading claims that readers report as spam. Write like a person and
the words barely matter.
