# Start Here

This is a manual for starting a reader newsletter from absolutely zero: no
audience, no budget, no tech background, and no idea where to click first. It
walks you through the whole thing with free tools only, and it stays honest
about what "free" really means on the internet.

It is written in the house style of everything Asdale Dynamics, LLC
publishes: explained like a friend would -- plain English, step by step,
never condescending. If a sentence assumes you already know something, that
is a bug, not a choice. Tell it back to us and we will fix it.

## Who this is for

- **Writers and authors** who want a direct line to the people who actually
  care about their work -- especially people building slowly, part-time,
  around a day job.
- **Anyone tired of renting an audience** from a social feed, where the
  number of people who see what you post is decided by someone else's
  algorithm each morning.
- **Beginners in general.** Every chapter assumes zero newsletter
  experience, and the glossary in [chapter 10](10-glossary.md) defines every
  piece of jargon the moment the chapters lean on it.

## What this is not

- **Not a growth-hacking manual.** Chapter 8 is about growing slower and
  keeping every reader you actually earn.
- **Not medical, legal, or financial advice**, and (small important point)
  the AI chapter is about writing with AI, not about doing anything with
  reader medical details -- never post private health information anywhere.
- **Not affiliated with any provider.** Chapter 2 recommends free options
  with their catches stated plainly, and no affiliate links anywhere.

## A five-minute quick start

If you want proof this can be done with free tools in under an hour:

1. Read [Why a Newsletter?](01-why-newsletters.md) (chapter 1, or skim it --
   10 minutes).
2. Open [Picking a Free Provider](02-choose-a-provider.md) (chapter 2), run
   the short decision list at the top, and make an account on the one it
   points to (20 minutes).
3. Copy [welcome email #1](../templates/welcome-sequence-template.md) out of
   the templates folder, change the parts in brackets, and paste it into your
   provider's "welcome email" setting (15 minutes).
4. Put your signup link in the one place readers already find you -- your
   book page, blog, or profile bio (5 minutes).

That is a working newsletter. The rest of the manual makes it *better*:
better subject lines (chapter 5), drafting help that still sounds like you
(chapter 6), a sending rhythm that fits a real life with a day job (chapter
7), honest growth (chapter 8), and a local archive on your own hard drive
(chapter 9).

## How the manual is organized

- **Front matter** (this page): orientation and the quick start.
- **Chapters 1 through 9**: the manual proper, in the order you will need
  them. Cross-references like "chapter 4's table" always mean the chapter
  numbers in the filenames.
- **Reference** (chapters 10 and 11): the glossary and a troubleshooting
  page for the moments when something just quietly breaks.
- **Templates**: copy-paste starting drafts
  ([welcome sequence](../templates/welcome-sequence-template.md),
  [issue template](../templates/issue-template.md),
  [subject-line cheat sheet](../templates/subject-line-cheatsheet.md),
  [signup form copy](../templates/signup-form-copy.md), and the
  [issue calendar](../templates/issue-calendar.md)).

## Conventions used in these pages

- **Bold** marks exact buttons and settings ("the **Subscribers** tab").
- `monospace` marks filenames, folder names, and commands you type.
- Anything with a bracket and a blank -- like `[your book's name]` -- is a
  slot to fill in when you copy a template.
- Money figures are USD and were last reviewed in **October 2026**. Free
  tiers change; each tool entry in chapter 2 carries a plain-speaking
  "honest limits" note and a recheck date, in the same spirit as the
  sibling project [free-author-marketing-kit](https://github.com/dylanasdale).

## Where this handbook got its bias

From three beliefs, stated once here so you can skip the rest if you reject
them: a small owned audience beats a large rented one; free should mean
understood, not sneaky; and a newsletter that ships on a boring, human
schedule beats a perfect one that never ships. Everything after this page
is those three beliefs, applied.
