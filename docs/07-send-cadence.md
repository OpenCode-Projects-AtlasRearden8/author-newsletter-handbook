# Send Without Dread

> Last reviewed: October 2026. Cadence advice is timeless and job-shaped,
> which is the point: this chapter is written for a person with a day
> job, a family, and a finite number of evening spoons.

Chapter 7 is the one experienced authors asked to be included. They did
not ask about providers or subject lines; they asked: *"How do I keep
doing this for years without it eating me alive?"* The dread answer
comes in three parts: a tiny promise, a repeatable pipeline, and
permission to be boring.

## First: make your promise tiny

The most common failure is not flaky writing; it is an over-promised
cadence. "Weekly!" lasts about nine weeks. Start with **monthly**. Once
a month, for a year, arrive reliably, beats weekly for two months and
then a silence so guilt-heavy the list feels radioactive (chapter 8's
"list hygiene" angle agrees: reliability trains readers; bursts train
them to skim you).

State the promise aloud in human terms, in every footer: "One issue a
month. That's the deal." Then the promise protects the work instead of
threatening it -- and every time you have two good ideas in one month,
banking the second one is *not breaking the promise* (it is the next
month's lead, written early).

## The pipeline: from blank to sent in four passes

The [issue template](../templates/issue-template.md) encodes this loop;
the calendar in [issue-calendar](../templates/issue-calendar.md) tracks
it. Names matter because names are what you put in a calendar. Four
passes:

| Pass   | Name      | Timebox    | What happens                                                        |
| ------ | --------- | ---------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1      | Capture   | all month  | jot ideas in one note, "swipe file" style; 2 minutes when inspired; no editing allowed |
| 2      | Outline   | 20 min     | pick the idea; write the one-sentence promise; ask AI for 3 outlines (chapter 6's loop) or hand-skeleton 3 bullets |
| 3      | Draft     | 45 min     | write ugly, fast, straight into the provider or a local file; nobody watch; no structure surgery |
| 4      | Edit + Send | 30 min   | read aloud once; kill canned lines (chapter 7's list lives in chapter 6); set subject + preheader; schedule |

Rough total: under two hours for a month's issue, in four separate
sittings you can do in a break room or before the house wakes. Monthly
means you never sprint; weekly means two of those sittings merge, and the
calendar below scales with you either way.

## Scheduling that fits a real life

- **Same day each month.** Let readers learn it: "first Tuesday." You'll
  get replies that actually expect it by month three.
- **Same time of day is folklore.** Tue-Thu morning slots are the
  long-standing sender convention; but reliability beats perfect
  timeslots by a lot. If 9pm fits your night, 9pm is best-in-class for
  you.
- **Schedule ahead once, not every month.** When issue N gets sent, put
  issue N+1's outline pass on the calendar *now*, at a plausible hour.
  Future-you is not to be trusted past two weeks.
- **The "don't miss twice" rule.** Life happens. If a month gets missed,
  the rule is not punishment, it is math: missing once is an event;
  missing twice is a new (worse) pattern. A two-sentence "we went quiet,
  here's the gem I owe you" says your absence, restarts the habit, and
  chapter 8's "no guilt pruning" thanks you for the honesty.
- **Batch the back-catalog.** Every welcome sequence (chapter 4) is a
  batch job -- after issue #1 exists, wrap one good line into that
  month's issue and let the catalog do the rest.

## Dread, specifically (because it's the real enemy)

- **Write to one person.** Name a real reader (or invent one: "the nurse
  whose schedule is like mine"). Drafts stop being performances; they
  become letters. Letters are easier to send than issues.
- **Lower the bar to "one good issue."** One good hook, one useful
  thing, one link. A "minimum viable issue" that ships on time beats an
  amazing one that never does. (The issue template enforces this with
  three checkboxes.)
- **It is not your guilt; it is arithmetic.** Twelve readers at month one
  means at most twelve possible reactions -- sending is how you find out
  any of them care. The data of not sending is perfectly silent.
- **De-personalize the misses.** Unsubscribes and soft bounces are not
  rejections; leaves fall in October. One monthly data session
  (10 minutes), never mid-writing.
- **The dread of writing to nobody.** If the list is genuinely tiny,
  chapter 8 has the fix: choose one public spot and pour one honest
  invite into it each month. Small lists with real connection beat
  medium lists with dead air; and 108 readers is already
  "real newsletter" in every practical sense.

## The three-month rescue plan

If a newsletter has been stalled for a while and the guilt has multiplied
beyond what a restart note can wave off:

1. Send a two-line "I owe you one" issue with your single best old link,
   restarted from chapter 9's backups, not a promise of future brilliance.
2. Recommit publicly to exactly the tiny promise (monthly).
3. Put the next *three* outline passes on the calendar in one sitting --
   outlines are the cheap, dread-resistant unit, and finishing three
   outlines bankrolls the next three sends.
