# Picking a Free Provider (Honestly)

> Costs and limits below were last reviewed in **October 2026**. Free tiers
> change often and without much fanfare -- recheck the pricing page the
> week you actually sign up. The "recheck" spirit comes from the sibling
> project free-author-marketing-kit; its `docs/03-free-newsletter-options.md`
> covers the same territory from the marketing angle.

A newsletter provider (industry word: an **ESP**, email service provider)
is the company that stores your subscriber list, sends your email, handles
unsubscribes for you, and keeps you off the worst spam lists. You cannot
legally or practically just BCC people from your own inbox. You need a
provider. Good news: for the size list a first-time author has, every
provider below is genuinely free.

## What "free" honestly means online

Three different things hide under the same word. Keep them apart or you
will feel cheated later.

- **Free forever.** No billing ever unless you want extra features. (Substack
  until you take paid subscriptions; MailerLite and Kit below a subscriber
  cap.)
- **Free tier.** Free below a subscriber or send limit; billing starts
  quietly when you cross it. (Almost every provider here.)
- **Free trial.** Free for a short window; wants the paid plan soon. (Skip
  these for newsletters.)

The honest label for every entry below is **free tier** unless the entry
says otherwise. And every provider has a catch on free tiers -- usually a
small footer tag reading "sent via [provider]" or limits on who can see
your fancy design tools. We state each catch in plain words, because a
catch that surprises you later is worse than a catch you accepted on day
one.

## The one test that matters: the exit door

Before you commit to *any* provider, ask two questions and verify both by
reading the help docs (10 minutes, no account needed):

1. **Can you export your full subscriber list** -- names plus emails, as
   CSV -- without emailing support? (The golden ticket. Test it with your
   own address in week one.)
2. **Can you get copies of the emails you sent** -- for your local archive,
   per chapter 9?

A provider that answers yes to both is safe to love. Answer no to either
and walk away regardless of how pretty the site is. Your list is yours;
the provider is a tenant.

## A decision path, not a decision

Answer these four questions in order:

1. **"I want the least thinking possible."** Substack. You press publish
   and it is simultaneously an email, a post page, and an archive. Catch
   restated: design is theirs, growth tools are theirs, and taking money
   means a fee cut. Fine trade for many writers. (If you want Substack's
   ease *and* your own site: MailerLite.)
2. **"I want a normal, boring, designed-by-me newsletter."** MailerLite.
   Generous free tier, real automation for the welcome sequence in chapter
   4, forms you can embed anywhere.
3. **"I am audience-first and might build big."** Kit. The ~10,000-
   subscriber free cap is a runway, not a trial; the creator ecosystem is
   active.
4. **"I write essays and care about typography and portability."**
   Buttondown. Knowingly tiny free cap; you are paying in subscribers
   rather than dollars to start, and the paid tiers are cheap for what
   they are.

Every path is legitimate. Do not let anyone (including this manual) make
you feel a provider choice is fate. **Chapter 9's archive ritual means
every provider here is escapable.**

## Things beginners do not know to check

Read the help page for each of these before you pick, not after:

- **Double opt-in support.** Asks new subscribers to click a confirmation
  link. Slightly more friction, dramatically cleaner list health. On by
  default in the EU; optional elsewhere. Recommended on.
- **GDPR consent checkbox** on signup forms. If any reader might be in the
  EU, you want a form with an explicit consent option; all providers here
  have it.
- **Custom sending domain.** Free plans often send from
  `yourname@provider.tld` or a provider subdomain. Custom domains are
  commonly a paid feature -- chapter 3 shows the one free workaround that
  matters (verifying your account thoroughly and writing a real reply-to).
- **Referral Tree, recommendations, and boosts** -- growth features are
  the classic "free-ish until useful" zone. Ignore them at first
  (chapter 8 is about honest channels, which need nothing paid).
- **Footer customization.** Check what the free plan lets you put at the
  bottom of your mail. Some providers lock the badge; some only require
  the legal bits (a physical mailing address, see chapter 3).

## What we recommend for an author starting today

Default recommendation: **MailerLite** (fit to plain-and-boring, generous
free tier, supports everything chapters 3-7 ask you to build). If you
would rather avoid trying to look designed until somebody pays you to
care: **Substack**. If your plan is "write nice text emails to a few
hundred people I know personally": **Buttondown** at first, export, and
re-decide at 80 subscribers.

Real numbers from the field, phrased as ranges: a first year of an author
newsletter commonly lives between 50 and 1,000 subscribers. Every free
tier above comfortably covers it. Cross a cap and your CSV export gets you
a free move -- the same ritual as the archiving chapter.
